Case study 01 · Logistics software

Dispatch, rebuilt around the exceptions

Client
Northgate Haulage
Year
2025 – 2026
Role
Lead product designer, 0–1
Team
2 designers, 5 engineers, 1 planner
Scope
Research, interaction, UI, design system

The problem was not the screen

Northgate's dispatchers opened the product at 4:40 a.m. to a screen of 214 rows. Every row was a truck that was on time. The four that were not were somewhere in the middle, and the dispatcher knew it only because the loading-bay colleague shouted.

What we changed

We spent three weeks on the floor before drawing anything. Two changes did almost all of the work: the board now sorts exceptions to the top and says why, and a run is only “open” once it has a named owner and a phone number that has been answered.

Everything else — the map, the load history, the driver notes — moved behind the run itself, where it is read far less often but is never more than two taps away.

Metric 11 minutes → 90 seconds Median time to identify a late run
Metric 214 rows → 3 cards Anything not exceptional moved out of view
Result Deployed to 19 depots No retraining, no release-notes backlash

Case study 02 · Clinical hardware

Vitals: a bedside chart that fits on a wall

Client
St Aidan's ward, Leeds
Year
2024
Role
Design lead
Scope
Print system, glyphs, bedside study

Nobody asked for a tablet

The ward had been issued tablets and quietly stopped using them: gloves, bed rails, thirty-second battery, no charging point. The paper chart had survived because paper is still the fastest thing in the room.

So we designed a chart

One A3 sheet per twelve hours. Numerals at 46 point, colour reserved entirely for “this number is wrong”. We tested it with night staff at the bedside rather than in a usability lab, and cut a third of what we first drew.

Metric 41 → 14 Fields on the sheet after two rounds of testing
Result In use on four wards Printed nightly by the ward clerk, four minutes' work

About

I design for people who cannot look down

Most of my work is in places where the user's hands are busy, cold, gloved, or holding something heavy — and where the cost of a mis-tap is measured in a delayed truck or a missed dose. That constraint is a gift. It removes most of the questions designers usually get to enjoy.

Before going independent I led design at Northgate Haulage and before that shipped clinical software at a company you have heard of. I prototype in whatever medium answers the question fastest, which is usually paper and a stopwatch.
