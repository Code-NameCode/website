About me

A designer who likes paper more than pixels, but does pixels too.

I came to editorial through a two-year archive job digitising a local newspaper's back issues, which is a slower way to learn a design system than drawing one from scratch, and a better one. I've typeset catalogues, annual reports, a novel, a poetry pamphlet, and the menu for a restaurant that has since closed.

These days I work mostly on print and long-form digital: research reports that need to be read as carefully as they're written, museum guides, and publications with a stubborn grid. I teach one day a fortnight at the Bristol School of Art.

  • Based in Bristol, working across UK and EU time zones
  • Available for commissions from March 2026
  • Two-day minimum. I do not do rush jobs and stop pretending otherwise.
  • Happy to be paid properly for the same work twice as often

A short history, loosely kept

reconstructed from memory, unfortunately

  1. 2014 start

    Archive assistant, Vale Weekly

    Eighteen months digitising back issues. Learned more about grids, gutters and how a two-column page fails at 380px than any course taught me.

  2. 2016

    In-house designer, Watershed Press

    Nine small books a year, a proofing workflow held together with spreadsheets and goodwill. Left when they bought a system that suited them more than me.

  3. 2018 Lisbon

    Two years, freelance, mostly abroad

    Worked for publishers in Lisbon and Porto. Learned to quote properly, to say no in writing, and that some projects are worth the rate being uncomfortable.

  4. 2020

    Back to Bristol; teaching begins

    Started taking one day a fortnight at the School of Art. The students are better at this than I was, and they correct me often, which is the point.

  5. 2026 now

    Independent, four commissions a year

    Deliberately small list: research reports, museum publications, and the occasional poster nobody else wanted. This scrapbook is the front door.

Structure before beauty

Get the reading order right on a grey page with no type. If it's boring to lay out with text boxes, no typeface will save it.

Proof it on paper

Every project gets one printed proof. Screens lie about weight, and readers can feel the difference between 80gsm and 120 even when they can't name it.

Fewer, better typefaces

Usually two, occasionally three. I have a folder of typefaces I don't use, which is the most useful folder on this machine. InDesignGlyphsOggRisoLaTeXCSS

Accessible from the first sketch

Contrast, reading order and a sensible tag structure are decisions made on the first page, not patched in the last week. It is much less work that way.
