Cover story · 14,000 words

The Long Accounting: what forty years of deferred maintenance actually cost

Across the western network, a generation of engineers has been told to spend nothing and repair everything. We followed six bridges, one hospital and a district heating loop to find out who is paying for that decision — and how the bill has been kept out of sight.

Words Ines Hovland Data Jonas Ek Photographs Archive, National Transport Board Published 14 September 2026

The short version

Deferred maintenance is not an accounting problem. It is a decision about who survives the winter. Six case studies, one uncomfortable chart, and a proposal for a maintenance levy that no minister has yet been willing to table.

The first thing Ines Hovland was told when she rang the district heating operator in Mo i Rana was that there was no story. The loop had been running since 1981. It had never failed. The system was, in the words of the duty engineer, “a solved problem”.

Six months of records later, the picture is different. The loop is not solved; it is understood, which is a different thing. Of 412 kilometres of district main, 148 have no recorded condition survey from this decade. Of the 61 substations, 23 are past the manufacturer’s stated service life. Nobody in the organisation disputes this, and nobody can say what it will cost to fix, because the modelling was never done and the budget line is not in the plan.

Section oneA policy of spending nothing

It would be easy to describe this as neglect. The documents tell a more precise story. Between 1994 and 2011, the board minutes record a consistent instruction: capital expenditure on the existing network would be held “at replacement level”, a phrase that on the accounts means “whatever the budget happens to be”. Successive chairs read that phrase aloud and then approved it. Nobody in the room disagreed, because the consequences of disagreement were a line crossing a department boundary.

What follows from that is a specific kind of blindness. A capital programme is a list of things you can point at. A maintenance programme is a list of things that continue not to happen. The second list is invisible in every dashboard the organisation owns, and the department that holds it has no constituency.

A note on our sources

Every figure in this article is drawn from published board minutes, the National Transport Board’s condition register, and four on-site visits between February and August 2026. Where an operator declined to give a figure, we say so rather than substitute an estimate. Two requests are still outstanding at the time of going to press; they are flagged below.

“Capital is a project with a name and a photograph. Maintenance is a line in a ledger that nobody is promoted for keeping. That asymmetry is the whole problem, and it is an organisational problem, not a money problem.” Dr Anneli Sundqvist, Nordic Institute for Infrastructure Policy, interviewed 3 June 2026

Section twoThe chart nobody published

Reconstructing the cost is possible, and it is more interesting than the figures the boards preferred. Set the condition register against the outturn maintenance spend and a shape emerges: the spend is flat in cash terms, which sounds like stability, but the network is twenty-two years older, so flat cash is a real decline of roughly two and a half per cent a year in the resources available per kilometre.

The gap is not a cliff. That is what makes it easy to ignore. Each year the shortfall is a few per cent, and each few per cent is small enough that no single board can be blamed for it. But small annual shortfalls compound against a physical asset that is not divisible, and the composite indices show a network that has crossed from “aging” into “deferred” sometime around 2016.

Section threeSix bridges, one pattern

We examined six structures on the same corridor, chosen because their paperwork was complete enough to compare. The physical condition differs — two are in reasonable repair, one is a candidate for replacement within the decade — but the administrative pattern is identical in all six.

Each has a risk register. Each register has an owner. In four of the six cases the named owner had changed role twice since the entry was written, and the register had not been re-signed. In five of six, the interim mitigation recorded in 2019 was a monitoring commitment; in three of those, the monitoring frequency specified has since been reduced without a decision being minuted.

What the pattern is not

It is not corruption, and it is not incompetence. Every engineer we spoke to could describe the failure mode of their own structure in detail. The problem is that the system has no way to convert that knowledge into a claim on money, and no reward for the effort of trying.

Section fourWhat a levy would cost

The proposal in the report, which we support with one reservation, is straightforward: a per-vehicle-kilometre charge on the strategic network, ring-fenced for condition work, with the schedule published and the spend reported annually against it. A road-user levy already exists for tunnel crossings; extending it to a maintenance account is an administrative change more than a political one.

Our reservation is about the reservation. Ring-fencing protects money from being reallocated, which is the point, but it also protects the programme from being reprioritised when the evidence changes. A levy with a five-year review built in is better than no levy. A levy that becomes another fixed line in a fixed plan is how this article's subject is created in the first place.

Section fiveNotes on sources

Four of the six bridges are named, with the operators’ agreement. Two are anonymised at their request. The district heating operator in Mo i Rana has not yet answered our request for the modelled replacement cost and the data is marked “outstanding” in every table it appears in. The Mo i Rana case is the one to watch.

Dispatch

04:12

The train that ran at 04:12 for 1,100 kilometres

It is worth being precise about what was restored in 2024, because the popular account is wrong in an interesting way. The Vienna night service was not “brought back” — it never stopped running at all. What changed is that it acquired a name, a booking system, and a number.

A number turns a service into a product. A product can be sold, audited, complained about and eventually improved, which is precisely the sequence that infrastructure of this kind has been missing for thirty years. Whether the people who fought for the number will like what happens next is another question, and one worth a longer piece than this.
