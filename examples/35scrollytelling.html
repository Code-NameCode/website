01 — 0 m

The surface is a skin, and it is the part we ruined

The top 26 metres warmed 1.9 °C above the pre-1990 average this summer. It is thin — a rounding error against 2 800 metres — and it is the only part anyone argued about.

18.4 °C · the warmest surface reading at this station since 1997

02 — 40 m

The mixed layer is the engine, and it is running late

Wind stirs the top 40 metres into one uniform slab. This year the slab formed 19 days later than the 1981–2010 mean, so the whole heat season went into that thinner skin rather than being spread down.

16.1 °C · mixed layer 19 days late · 3.4 °C below the seasonal mean at 60 m

03 — 1 400 m

Below here the water has not been to the surface in centuries

The thermocline is a lid, 800 metres thick, and it is the reason the surface can change without the rest of the ocean caring. Below it, temperature falls at roughly one degree per thousand metres — the oldest water in the Atlantic basin, formed in the far north.

4.1 °C · no direct observation of this layer since the 2019 cast

04 — 2 400 m

Something is releasing heat in the deep, and it is not us

At 2 400 metres the float logged 0.31 °C above the modelled value for this depth — outside the range of any previous cast. We do not yet have a cause. Hydrothermal seep from the fracture zone 30 km north is the working theory.

0.31 °C above model · flagged 3 November · still unexplained

Station geometry

Why the float had to go down at all

Satellites see the surface. Buoys see the top few hundred metres. Everything below that has been guesswork built on 1970s-era hydrographic survey lines.

The profile below is the actual bottom under the station, from the 2019 multibeam survey. The float's parking depth sits 340 metres off the shelf break, which is why the cable stayed quiet for so long.

Honest limits

Three things we still cannot see

This story is eleven casts from one place. Here is what that does and does not mean.
