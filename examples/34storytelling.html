The Kessel report · 2016 – 2024

The river that ran brown, and the nine years that brought it back

In 2016 the Kessel carried 40 tonnes of eroded topsoil past the town each spring. This is the story of what happened next, told in the order it happened.

Chapter one

A river we lost in about six years

Nobody in Ardvale voted to lose the Kessel. It happened the way most losses happen: one field boundary at a time, each change reasonable on its own.

Between 2009 and 2015 the arable blocks above Ardvale were ploughed to the hedge line. Hedgerows came out for a 4.6 m sprayer. The slope above the town lost the roots that had held it together since the last Ice Age, and the first serious rain moved what had been a field into a river.

We did not notice the loss. We noticed the flood.

The March 2016 spate carried 40 tonnes of topsoil through the town in eleven hours. It closed the mill bridge twice and put 6 cm of silt in the Ardvale treatment works. The river was carrying the shape of the fields above it.

Chapter two

Nine years, measured one field at a time

Restoration is not a project. It is a queue. The only question the programme ever asked was which field goes into the queue next.

We started at the bottom and worked uphill, because planting at the top of a catchment while the silt keeps arriving just moves the problem down. Fourteen hundred hectares were taken out of tillage, 9.2 km of hedgerow went back in, and 61 000 trees went up across eleven winters.

Chapter three

Marta Espinal, who farms the worst field

The largest field in the programme belonged to someone who did not want to be in the programme.

They kept offering me money to take the field out. I kept saying the field pays the mortgage. Nobody asked me what the field would look like in ten years.

Her 61 hectares sat directly above the worst of the silt fans, and she was right about the money. The breakthrough came when nobody sent a letter at all: a Riverkeeper field officer turned up in April with a spade and a hedgerow, and they planted 900 metres of it together over three Sundays.

Marta took the 61 hectares out of tillage in 2019 for a 15-year agreement at a rent she calls "stupid, but written down". She now runs two cattle and sells the hedge. Her son runs the new cover on the lower field.

Marta Espinal, Ardvale

Chapter four

What we did, in the order we did it

Five decisions, taken over nine years. Most of them were boring, and the boring ones are the ones that worked.

  1. 2016 – 17

    Stop the source before touching the river

    Two hundred and forty hectares under permanent cover, chosen by slope rather than by willingness. We took the steepest ground first, which is also the ground nobody wanted to farm.

  2. 2017 – 18

    Put the boundary back

    9.2 km of new hedgerow, planted to the original 1780 line where the parish survey still records it. Existing hedges were cut on a two-year rotation, not every year, which is the single most common mistake.

  3. 2018 – 20

    Reconnect the floodplain

    Eleven hectares of bank lowered and set back to let the river out of its ditch. Two footbridges replaced, because a lower bank you cannot cross is a flood defence you have simply moved.

  4. 2020 – 22

    Plant the wood

    61 000 trees, mostly willow and alder on the lower floodplain, hazel and oak on the terraces. Dogged willow in blocks, not rows, so the deer can browse the edge without stripping the middle.

  5. 2022 – 24

    Pay for the next ten years

    Fourteen agreements signed through to 2034, so the maintenance is somebody's job and not a charity's.

Chapter five

What it added up to

These are the numbers in the 2024 annual report. They are measured, not modelled, unless the caption says otherwise.

The next ten years need 1.1 million more

We have agreements to 2034 on the ground we have already done. We do not have agreements on the twelve fields above the new wood, and without those the wood is a very expensive pile of trees with nothing to slow the water when it comes out of it.

£1.94m raised since 2016. £1.1m outstanding. Every agreement signed so far has been paid for in full.

Chapter six

Where you come in

Planting days run from October to March, Wednesdays and Saturdays, 09:30 to 15:00, meet at the Ardvale mill car park. We provide tools, waders, tea and a great deal of unsolicited advice from retired contractors.

If you would rather put money in than a day of it, choose the fund option. If you own or farm ground above the Kessel, tell us and we will come and walk it with you before anyone writes anything down.
